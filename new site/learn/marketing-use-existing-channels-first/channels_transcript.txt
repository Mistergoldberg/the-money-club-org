Marketing: Use Existing Channels First

Location Location Location
Put the lemonade stand where people walk.
In module 7, Creative Fundamentals: Photography, Design & Visual Clarity, you built a five-second storefront.
You created a clear product photograph.
You wrote a strong headline.
You explained the offer.
You showed the price.
You added a call to action and a reason to trust you.
Now the lemonade stand looks ready.
But there is still one problem.
What if you put it on an empty street?
The product could be useful.
The poster could be clear.
The price could be fair.
The lemonade could be cold.
But if nobody walks past, nobody buys.
That is why distribution matters.
Distribution is how an offer reaches the people who might buy it.
Marketing is not only about making messages.
It is also about deciding where those messages should appear.
You do not always need to build an audience from scratch.
Customers already gather in marketplaces, schools, community groups, local businesses, parent networks, email lists, retail stores, and social platforms.
The operator’s job is to find the right crowd, earn permission, and put the offer where people already walk.
The Empty-Street Problem
Imagine two lemonade stands.
The first stand has a beautiful sign.
The table is clean.
The cups are lined up.
The price is easy to see.
But the stand is at the end of a quiet street.
Only three people walk past all day.
The second stand has a simple sign.
It is placed beside a busy park on a hot afternoon.
Families are already walking past.
Children are already thirsty.
People can see the lemonade, understand the price, and buy it immediately.
Which stand has the better marketing?
Probably the second one.
The sign still matters.
The product still matters.
The price still matters.
But location changes the opportunity.
A marketing channel is a place or system that carries your offer to potential customers.
A channel could be physical:
A school bulletin board
A community centre
A local store
A neighbourhood event
A flyer delivered to nearby homes
A channel could also be digital:
An online marketplace
A parent email list
A community group
A social platform
An influencer’s account
A local business newsletter
The channel is not just where people see the message.
It is the route that takes someone from seeing the offer to buying it.
For example:
School newsletter → parent reads the offer → parent scans the QR code → parent visits the order page → parent pre-orders
That is a conversion path.
A conversion happens when someone takes the action you wanted.
They might click.
They might reply.
They might sign up.
They might place a pre-order.
They might buy.
The best channel is not always the one with the largest audience.
A social platform might reach thousands of people.
But most of them may live far away or have no need for your product.
A school newsletter might reach only 300 families.
But those families may live nearby, trust the school, and fit the offer.
The smaller channel could produce more customers.
That is channel fit.
Channel fit means the people, message, offer, and buying path belong together.
Ask:
Are the right people already here?
Does our offer make sense in this place?
Can someone move easily from seeing it to buying it?
For a local household restock kit, one million random views may be less useful than 100 nearby families.
Attention only matters when it reaches someone who might act.
Find the Existing Foot Traffic
Different channels contain different people.
They also provide different amounts of trust, speed, reach, and cost.
The goal is not to use every channel.
The goal is to understand what each one can do.
Marketplaces
Marketplaces contain people who are already looking at products.
They might be searching by category, price, location, or need.
This creates buying intent.
Buying intent means the person may already be interested in making a purchase.
The advantage is that the marketplace has already gathered the shoppers.
The disadvantage is that many sellers may be competing for the same customer.
The marketplace may also charge fees, control how products appear, or make it difficult for a new seller to stand out.
Retailers
Retailers already have customers, shelves, checkout systems, and physical traffic.
A local retailer might agree to display a student product, accept pre-orders, or host a small pop-up.
The retailer can add trust because customers already know the store.
But the retailer may expect part of the selling price.
It may also have rules about packaging, insurance, inventory, returns, or product quality.
Access to a retailer’s audience usually comes with conditions.
Schools
Schools contain students, parents, teachers, and families connected to a specific community.
A school newsletter, event, bulletin board, or parent council can provide focused local reach.
Schools may also provide trust.
But students must get permission before using school communication channels.
A school email list belongs to the school.
It is not a free advertising list.
The message also needs to fit the school’s purpose and rules.
Community Groups
Neighbourhood associations, recreation groups, cultural organizations, religious communities, sports teams, and local online groups bring people together around a shared place or interest.
These groups may be useful because members already have something in common.
A neighbourhood group could be a strong fit for a locally delivered household product.
But community groups are built on relationships.
A student should ask permission, explain the program honestly, and avoid posting the same sales message repeatedly.
Trust can open a channel.
Spam can close it.
Social Platforms
Social platforms provide attention.
A photograph, short video, post, or demonstration can spread quickly.
They are useful for showing how a product works, telling the founder’s story, or sharing the results of a test.
But attention is not the same as demand.
Likes do not always become orders.
Followers do not always live nearby.
A large number of views can feel exciting while producing no customers.
Measure actions, not applause.
Partnerships
A partnership happens when two people or organizations help each other reach a shared goal.
A student selling a bicycle care kit might partner with a local bike shop.
A student selling a plant starter kit might partner with a garden club.
A household restock business might partner with a neighbourhood organization or delivery service.
A useful partnership can combine:
An audience
A physical location
Trust
Expertise
Delivery
Promotion
Shared costs
The partnership should create value for both sides.
Do not only ask:
“What can this partner do for us?”
Also ask:
“What can we do for them?”
Local Businesses
Local businesses already have customers walking through their doors.
A café might display a flyer.
A shop might host a product card.
A community business might include the offer in its newsletter or let students run a weekend pop-up.
The strongest approach is specific and respectful:
“We are running a student business test for local families. Could we display this flyer for one week and track how many orders come through its QR code?”
That is easier to evaluate than:
“Can you help market our business?”
Flyers and Posters
Flyers and posters place a message in a specific physical location.
They can work well when the product is local and the location matches the customer.
A household kit poster near a community noticeboard may make sense.
The same poster placed somewhere with no family traffic may not.
Each flyer or poster should include:
A clear offer
A visible price
A simple call to action
A QR code or contact method
An honest trust signal
A way to identify where the response came from
A different QR code or order code for each location can help measure which poster worked.
Email Lists
Email can reach people directly.
It can explain the offer, show the product, provide the price, and link to an order page.
But access matters.
Students should use lists they have permission to contact.
They should not collect, copy, or use private email addresses without consent.
A strong email is relevant, clear, and easy to act on.
It does not need to tell the entire business story.
Parent Networks
Parent networks may be one of the strongest first channels for a student business.
Parents know other parents.
They participate in schools, teams, clubs, neighbourhood groups, and community activities.
The trust already exists.
That makes it easier to run a small test and get useful feedback.
But students should not treat relationships as guaranteed sales.
The offer still needs to solve a problem.
The price still needs to make sense.
The customer still gets to say no.
Influencers
An influencer has an audience that pays attention to what they share.
The influencer does not need to be famous.
A local coach, teacher, organizer, student leader, parent, or neighbourhood creator might have a small but relevant audience.
This is sometimes more useful than a large general audience.
The important questions are:
Does the audience match the customer?
Does the influencer actually trust the offer?
Will the promotion feel honest?
What will the influencer expect in return?
An endorsement should never pretend to be independent if someone was paid or given a free product.
Trust depends on honesty.

You do not need to place the lemonade stand on every corner.

Calculate the Cost per Customer
A channel can feel inexpensive while still producing expensive customers.
Cost per customer tells you how much you spent to gain each new customer.
The basic formula is:
Cost per customer = total channel cost ÷ number of new customers
If you spend $30 printing flyers and gain three customers:
$30 ÷ 3 = $10 per customer
If you spend $20 on a community event table and gain ten customers:
$20 ÷ 10 = $2 per customer
The second channel produced customers at a lower cost.
But do not look at cost alone.
Ask whether those customers were profitable.
If the business earns only $4 in gross profit from each sale, spending $10 to acquire a customer does not work unless that customer buys again.
You should also track the time required.
A free channel that takes 12 hours to manage may not truly be free.
Run Small Channel Tests
Do not commit the full marketing budget immediately.
Run a small test in each of the top three channels.
For example:
Channel Test
Small First Move
What to Measure

Give each test a clear time period.
Use the same offer when possible.
Track where every response came from.
Then compare the results.

This produces evidence.
One channel may create many views but no orders.
Another may reach fewer people but produce five customers.
The second channel has stronger traction.
Students then decide:
Continue: The channel reaches the right people and produces customers at a workable cost.
Change: The channel has potential, but the message, offer, location, or conversion path needs improvement.
Stop: The channel produces weak results, costs too much, or reaches the wrong audience.
Stopping a weak channel protects the marketing budget.
It allows more time and money to move toward the channels that work.

A ranked list of possible channels
The top three channels to test first
A small test for each channel
A clear conversion path
A measurement plan
A decision to continue, change, or stop
In Module 7, you learned to make the offer understandable in five seconds.
Now you know where to put it.
The photograph earns attention.
The headline explains the benefit.
The channel supplies the audience, context, and trust.
The conversion path turns attention into action.
The measurement tells you whether the channel worked.
Do not build your lemonade stand and hope a crowd appears.
Find the crowd.
Earn permission.
Test the corner.
Measure what happens.
Then put more effort where people actually stop and buy.
Put the lemonade stand where people walk.
